The number one question I get from new owners is "why isn't my gecko eating?" and 90% of the time the answer is: they just got there. Give them two weeks.

A newly arrived gecko is stressed. New smells, new enclosure layout, new light cycle, you walking by every hour to check on them — it's overwhelming. I don't handle new arrivals for the first five days minimum, and I don't panic if they don't eat for the first week or two. I put food in and leave them alone. Almost every time, they start eating the moment they feel safe.

If it's been three weeks and they're still refusing food AND losing weight in the tail, that's when I'd look deeper (parasites, temp issue, impaction). But most of the time? They just need time.

**Example Post 2:**

Shed day is not photo day. I'm saying this because I used to make this mistake constantly.

When a gecko is in shed — you'll notice their skin going dull and their eyes turning blue-gray — they're temporarily vision-impaired and their skin is sensitive. They don't want to be handled, and handling them can cause the shed to stick in places it shouldn't. Toes are the big one. A piece of retained shed wrapped around a toe will cut off circulation within a few days. I've lost a toe or two on young animals before I figured this out.

What I do now: put a moist hide in the enclosure a few days before I see shed starting (coconut fiber works perfectly). Let them handle it themselves. Check the shed afterward — it should come off in one piece like a tiny inside-out sock. If it didn't, soak the toe in warm water and gently roll the retained shed off with a wet cotton swab. Never pull.

Your gecko will thank you by being absolutely ravenous the day after a good shed.

## 4. MORPH_EDUCATION

**Description:** Deep-dive genetics and morph education. This is where Sarah demonstrates genuine expertise and builds serious credibility with the hardcore hobbyist community.

**Tone Notes:**
- Get genuinely nerdy — this audience wants the real information
- Explain the "why" behind genetics, not just the "what"
- Use real terminology (codominant, polygenic, het, visual, combo) but explain it naturally
- Make it feel like a conversation with a knowledgeable friend, not a Wikipedia article
- Breeding projects and real examples ground abstract genetics in something tangible

**Example Post 1:**

Not all albinos are the same gene, and this matters a lot if you're planning a breeding project.

There are three distinct albino strains in leopard geckos: Tremper (Texas Strain), Rainwater (Las Vegas Albino), and Bell. They look similar but they're genetically incompatible — if you cross a Tremper Albino to a Rainwater Albino, you don't get double albinos. You get a bunch of hatchlings that look completely normal but are het for BOTH strains. That's not bad — it just means the magic happens in the next generation when you breed those hets back to each other.

I work exclusively with Tremper and Rainwater in my collection right now, and I keep them in completely separate breeding groups. Trempers tend to run warm: golden yellow tones, pink eyes, a slightly more active temperament in my experience. My Rainwaters are paler, sometimes with a lavender wash, smaller eyes. Both are beautiful in different ways.

If you're buying a het animal, always ask which strain. It matters for your project.

**Example Post 2:**

Everyone wants a Super Mack Snow but not everyone knows what they're actually buying into with this morph.

Mack Snow is a codominant mutation — meaning you can tell it's there even when the animal only carries one copy. A regular Mack Snow (one copy) has a whitish base color, reduced pattern contrast, and sometimes a slightly washed-out look compared to a normal. A Super Mack Snow (two copies) is something different entirely: born almost completely white, almost no visible pattern, sometimes faint speckling. They can look like a different species when they first hatch.

What makes Super Snows interesting as a breeding project is that if you breed two Mack Snows together, you get a predictable 1:2:1 ratio — roughly 25% normals, 50% Mack Snows, and 25% Super Mack Snows. You can plan around it. The Super Snows are usually the most visually striking animals in the clutch but the normals still have value as het projects depending on what else is in the parents.

I have two Super Mack Snow females from my spring clutches available now — they've started developing their adult pattern and it's going to be something. DM me if you want first look.
